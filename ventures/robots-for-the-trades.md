## The blunt version first

The idea is sound. The order you do it in decides whether it works.

Owning construction robots is not available to you right now, and the numbers say
so plainly:

| Machine | Price | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| SAM100 bricklayer | ~$500,000 | The maker shelved it |
| TyBot-class rebar tier | $200,000–$250,000 | Rents at $6,000–$8,000/mo |
| Hilti Jaibot | Not published | Reported $10,000+/mo to rent |
| Boston Dynamics Spot | $74,500 base, $100,000+ configured | Cheapest serious entry |
| Ekso EVO vest | ~$5,000/unit | Not really a robot |

### The three you've actually seen — and they price better

| Machine | Asset value | Rents for | Monthly yield |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Demolition robot** (Brokk BHB 455) | ~$140,000 replacement | **$12,500/mo** | **~8.9%** |
| **Painting robot** (Okibo, PaintGo class) | $10,000–$50,000 | Quote-based | — |
| **Autonomous retrofit** (Built Robotics Exosystem) | Not published | Rentable from 3 months | — |

Compare that to the rebar robot: $6,000–$8,000 a month on a $200,000–$250,000
machine is **2.2% to 3.5%** monthly. The demolition robot is roughly **three times
the yield** on a machine that costs half as much. Your eyes were better than my
spreadsheet.

**The catch, and it's a real one.** Good economics attract incumbents. Brokk already
runs a nationwide rental network, and equipment houses stock these machines. You
would not be opening a category — you'd be competing with established rental yards
that have fleets, service techs and transport. That doesn't kill the idea; it means
**ownership is not where you'd win**, at least not first.

**Where each one actually fits your plan:**

- **Demolition robot** — the proof that the rental model works, and the clearest
  thing to write about. People searching "demo robot rental Los Angeles" are ready to
  spend. Best Phase 1 content and Phase 2 referral target.
- **Painting robot** — at $10,000–$50,000, the only one you could realistically own
  without financing. If Phase 2 shows painters converting, this is the first machine
  to buy. Vendors claim payback in 12–18 months; treat that as unverified.
- **Autonomous Bobcat / excavator retrofit** — the most interesting story and the
  weakest ownership play. It's a kit the contractor bolts to *their own* machine,
  installs in hours and fully reverses. That makes it a referral product, not a
  rental asset. Great video content, though.

A $250,000 rebar robot renting at $6,000–$8,000 a month pays back in roughly
**3.6 to 5.7 years** at realistic utilisation — and that is before maintenance,
insurance, transport, storage and financing. You have debt to clear and about
15 hours a week. Buying a machine now would be the fastest way to kill this idea.

> The good news: **the part you're actually world-class at costs nothing.**

You rank construction companies #1 on Google in Los Angeles. You've done it for
eConstruct. The scarce thing in this market is not capital — plenty of investors
have that. It's **demand discovery**: knowing which trade in which metro will
actually pay for which machine. Nobody owns that data. You can.

## The three phases

### Phase 1 — Own the search and the audience (0 capital)

Build the site and the content. Rank for every way a contractor asks this question.

**Target searches** — one page per intent, per trade:
- "construction robot rental [city]"
- "rebar tying robot cost"
- "drywall robot for contractors"
- "layout robot vs crew"
- "is a construction robot worth it"

**Content that earns the ranking**
- Honest cost breakdowns. The real prices above, not vendor brochures.
- "Worth it or not" maths per trade, at real crew rates.
- Video. You already make AI video better than anyone in this niche does.

**Social, per craft, not one generic feed.** Concrete, framing, drywall, electrical,
masonry. Each trade thinks the others' problems are irrelevant. Speak to one at a time.

**The real output of Phase 1 is not traffic. It's a list.** Every lead that comes in
tells you: which trade, which city, which job, what they'd pay. That list is the asset.

### Phase 2 — Broker, don't buy (near-zero capital)

Robots already exist and their makers need customers. You have the customers.

- Referral or finder's agreements with the RaaS vendors (Dusty, HP, Hilti, Built).
- A "match me to a machine" intake on the site that routes a contractor to the right
  vendor — and pays you for it.
- Keep every enquiry. After 6 months you'll know the one machine with the deepest,
  most repeatable demand in LA.

This phase proves demand **with someone else's capital**. If nobody converts here,
you've learned the thesis is wrong for the price of some content. That's the cheapest
possible failure.

### Phase 3 — Own one machine (only if Phase 2 proves it)

Trigger to proceed: **you have turned away the same job three times** because you had
no machine. Not before.

## Funding: the ladder, in the order it actually works

You've said your credit is poor. That rules out the easy version of step 3 and makes
steps 1 and 2 not just "first" but **the entire plan for now**. The good news is that
neither of them needs credit, capital, or anyone's signature.

| Step | What it needs | Dilution | Credit check |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Revenue share with owners** | A phone call | None | **None** |
| **2. Vendor referral / channel** | An audience | None | **None** |
| **3. Equipment finance** | 680+ credit, or a co-signer at 720+ | None, but debt | **Yes** |
| **4. Outside capital** | A business that already works | Yes | Depends |

### Step 1 — Rent out somebody else's idle machine

This is the whole business with none of the capital. A demolition arm earning $12,500
a month is parked between jobs more often than its owner admits. You have the
contractor searching for one. Split the rental.

No credit, no debt, no asset to insure, and if it doesn't work you've lost a few calls.

### Step 2 — Get paid for the leads you're already generating

Roofing leads sell for $200+. HVAC installs run $50–$150. Exclusive leads go for two
to three times shared ones. A lead for a **$12,500-a-month rental** is worth more than
either, and you're the one ranking for it.

Vendors and rental yards both need customers in Los Angeles. Referral or co-marketing,
their paperwork, your audience.

### Step 3 — Equipment finance, and the honest warning

Equipment lenders care about the machine first, because the machine is the collateral.
They also want 680+ personal credit, and 700–720+ for anything zero-down. Below 650
usually means 10–20% down or a co-signer with 720+ and business history.

**Letters of intent from Phase 2 are what make this work.** Lenders will weigh signed
contracts and purchase orders. Twenty contractors saying "I'd rent that at this price"
is the strongest thing you can bring to that conversation — stronger than a pitch deck.

**About the co-signer.** It puts someone else's credit behind an asset that might sit
idle for months. If the rentals slow down, their score takes the hit, not just yours.
Don't ask anyone to sign until Phase 2 has produced real, repeated, paying demand —
and understand I'm not a financial advisor. Talk to someone who is before anyone signs.

### Step 4 — Outside capital, probably not

Angels fund software with a path to 10x. A machine renting in Los Angeles is a lending
proposition, not a venture one, and pitching it to angels burns months you don't have.

If outside money ever makes sense here, it's a **local partner who wants the asset**
while you bring the demand — not a Silicon Valley cheque.

**One legal note:** publicly advertising that you're seeking investors can count as
general solicitation under US securities law, which carries real requirements. The
partner page on the site is written as partnership conversations, not an offering.
Keep investment talks private and get a lawyer involved before money moves.

Then buy the one machine the data points at — probably the cheapest viable unit, not
the most impressive one — and rent it against the demand you already hold.

On today's numbers that is most likely **a painting robot**, at $10,000–$50,000 —
the only category reachable without good credit or a big loan. Demolition has better yield but established
competition; the retrofit kits aren't really ownable.

## Why this beats the obvious version

Most people start at Phase 3, buy a machine, then go looking for renters. They end up
with a $250,000 asset sitting in a yard between jobs. You'd be starting with the
demand and buying the asset last, which is the opposite risk profile and the only one
your balance sheet can carry.

---

## Honest risks

1. **Timing.** The robots that matter in 2028 are being built now. Anything bought in
   2026 carries obsolescence risk — another reason to be the media layer first.
2. **National rental chains are already here** in demolition. Brokk rents nationwide
   and equipment yards carry these machines. Your defence is the audience and the
   trade relationships, not the equipment.
3. **The labour-shortage thesis is widely repeated and loosely sourced.** Treat it as
   a hypothesis Phase 1 tests, not a fact you build on.
4. **Your own standing rule:** no new business until the portfolio makes $2,000/month.
   This sits in the 11 PM Club precisely because of that rule. Phase 2 is the earliest
   it should ask for real hours.
5. **Time.** You have four ventures and the Jarvis build already running. Phase 1 is
   content, which your agents can do — but only after they exist.
